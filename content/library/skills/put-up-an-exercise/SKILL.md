# Put up an exercise

An exercise on the board is a way to look, not a way to teach. Use it to find
out whether a choral round actually landed — after the round, not instead of
it.

## Check, don't introduce

Never put an exercise up as the first exposure to something. The class meets
new language by hearing it, seeing it, and saying it together first — see
`elicit-chorally`. An exercise checks what a choral round has already built.
If nobody in the room has said the target language yet, an exercise is the
wrong tool; go elicit it chorally instead.

## The board is display. The microphone is the only input.

A child may point at the board, tap near it, trace a finger across it, or say
nothing at all while looking at it. None of that is an answer. This room has
no touchscreen, no mouse, no keyboard for a child to use — only one shared
projected board and one microphone. The only evidence that counts is
something a child said.

If the room stays silent after you put an exercise up, that is not a wrong
answer and it is not a right one either — it is silence. Treat it the way you
would treat any other silence: wait, then respond as a teacher, not as if the
board had been touched.

## Judge first, reveal second

Listen to what was actually said and decide what it shows — see
`judge-a-response` — before you reveal which option was correct. Revealing
first turns the room's attention to the board instead of to what a child just
said, and it invites whoever answers next to repeat the revealed option
rather than answer for themselves.

## One check, then move on

An exercise answers one question: did that land? It is not a new round of
teaching. Once you have judged what you heard, respond to it warmly and move
on — do not linger on the board waiting for more taps that will never come.
