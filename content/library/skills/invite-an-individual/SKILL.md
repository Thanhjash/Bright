# Invite an individual

One child speaking alone is the most valuable evidence you can get and the most
expensive thing you can get wrong. A child embarrassed once in front of thirty
peers may not volunteer again for a term.

## Never before choral rehearsal

If the class has not said it together yet, you are not ready. Go to
`elicit-chorally`.

## Invite, never force

Ask for a volunteer first: *"Who would like to say hello to me?"*

The adult may point to a child who is comfortable. You do not select a child who
has shown reluctance, and you never insist.

## If they freeze

Do not wait. Do not ask again. Take it back to the group immediately and warmly:

> "That's okay. Let's all say it together!"

Then run one choral round and move on. Come back to that child later in a choral
round, where there is no spotlight — not with the same individual question.

## Ask the room first, then choose

Never name a child before the thinking starts. Ask everyone, leave the wait, and
only then choose who answers. Naming first turns twenty-nine children into an
audience.

## These are scarce — spend them deliberately

Individual turns are the only interactions that tell you about **one** child, and
there is room for roughly **eight to twelve in a lesson**. Everything else —
choral, signals, pairs, physical response — is teaching, and that is not a lesser
thing.

Choose the next child from what you can point at:

- who has not had a turn for the longest
- which objective has no individual evidence yet
- whose last attempt could not be judged
- which objective has only pointing, and needs a spoken one

Never choose because a child seems shy, weak, clever, or quiet. You do not know
that, and a turn handed out on a hunch is a judgement you cannot defend.

The children who most need to practise are the least likely to volunteer, so a
teacher who only ever talks to whoever waves has failed the other twenty-nine.

## Afterwards

Respond to the meaning, warmly, then move on. Praise the attempt, not the
accuracy — answer what they said as a person would, using their name.

Record evidence for what they actually did — see `judge-a-response`. Say nothing
about it out loud.
